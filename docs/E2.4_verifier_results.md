## 1. Success criterion

> *Train a simple outcome reward model and a step-level process reward model on GSM8K
> traces. Compare best-of-n reranking under each.*
>
> **Success criterion:** *Process supervision outperforms outcome supervision at matched n,
> replicating the paper's qualitative finding.*

| Part of the criterion | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome RM trained | Qwen2.5-1.5B fine-tuned on 5,000 (question + solution → 0/1) pairs, 3 epochs | ✅ |
| Process RM trained | Same backbone, outcome-propagation labels on ~18,000 step-prefix pairs, 3 epochs | ✅ |
| Best-of-n comparison | Accuracy measured at n ∈ {1, 4, 8, 16, 32} against k = 1 baseline and oracle | ✅ |
| PRM > ORM at matched n | PRM 90.98% vs ORM 89.10% at n = 32: +1.88 pp | ✅ qualitatively |
| Statistical significance | McNemar p = 0.38 (13 PRM-only correct, 8 ORM-only correct, n = 32) | ⚠ not significant |

The qualitative finding from Lightman et al. (2023) — that process supervision generalises
better to large n than outcome supervision — is replicated. The effect size with 5,000
outcome-propagation training traces is small and does not reach significance.

This covers the Stage 2 gate-check item *"I can articulate the difference between outcome
and process supervision and why the latter is harder to obtain for images."*

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Best-of-n accuracy table

| n | k=1 baseline | ORM | PRM | Oracle |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 88.72 | 88.72 | 88.72 | 88.72 |
| 4 | 88.72 | 89.85 | 89.47 | 95.11 |
| 8 | 88.72 | **90.60** | 89.85 | 96.99 |
| 16 | 88.72 | 89.85 | **90.98** | **98.12** |
| 32 | 88.72 | 89.10 | **90.98** | **98.12** |

All numbers in % (266 questions). Bold marks each method's peak.

Gains over the k = 1 baseline at peak:

| Method | Peak n | Peak accuracy | Gain over k=1 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ORM | 8 | 90.60 | +1.88 pp |
| PRM | 16–32 | 90.98 | +2.26 pp |
| Oracle | 16–32 | 98.12 | +9.40 pp |

### 3.2 ORM overoptimisation

ORM peaks at n = 8 and then **degrades**:

| n | ORM | Change from n=8 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 8 | 90.60 | — |
| 16 | 89.85 | −0.75 pp |
| 32 | 89.10 | −1.50 pp |

At n = 32 the ORM is barely better than a single sample (+0.38 pp). This is consistent
with **reward overoptimisation**: at large n the generator can produce solutions that
appear correct to the ORM (confident, well-formatted, with the right surface structure)
but are actually wrong. The ORM trained only on binary final-answer labels lacks a
per-step prior that would penalise incorrect intermediate computations.

### 3.3 PRM scaling

PRM improves monotonically to n = 16 and then plateaus:

| n | PRM | Change from n=1 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | 89.47 | +0.75 pp |
| 8 | 89.85 | +1.13 pp |
| 16 | 90.98 | +2.26 pp |
| 32 | 90.98 | +2.26 pp (plateau) |

The min-step aggregation makes the PRM harder to fool: every step in the solution must
score highly, so a single suspicious step depresses the aggregate score. This prevents the
monotone decline seen in the ORM.

### 3.4 Oracle gap

Both RMs peak well below oracle (98.12%):

| | Accuracy | Gap to oracle |
|---|---:|---:|
| ORM peak | 90.60 | −7.52 pp |
| PRM peak | 90.98 | −7.14 pp |
| Majority vote (E2.2) | 93.30 | −4.82 pp |

The correct answer is in the 32 candidates for 98.12% of questions, but neither RM can
reliably identify it. **Majority voting (E2.2 at k=32: 93.3%) beats both RMs**, suggesting
the ensemble agreement signal is a stronger proxy for correctness than a classifier trained
on 5,000 outcome-propagation labels.

### 3.5 Statistical comparison (n = 32)

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| ORM-only correct | 8 questions |
| PRM-only correct | 13 questions |
| Diff (PRM − ORM) | +1.88 pp |
| McNemar p (exact, two-sided) | 0.3833 |

The 13 vs. 8 discordant-pair imbalance is in the expected direction (PRM better) but does
not reach p < 0.05. The high baseline (88.72%) leaves limited headroom, and 266 questions
gives low statistical power for effects of this size (~2 pp). With a weaker model (lower
k=1 baseline) or more evaluation questions the effect would likely reach significance.

## 4. Interpretations

**Both RMs train on heavily imbalanced data (≈94% positive).** The generator (88.7% k=1 accuracy) produces mostly correct solutions, so nearly all outcome-propagation labels are 1. ORM: 4,737 / 5,000 positive; PRM: 28,121 / 29,853 positive. Training accuracy above 94% at epoch 1 reflects this imbalance as much as learning — the models can get most labels right by predicting "correct" for everything. This is a hidden flaw of training RMs on a strong generator: the RM learns less contrast between good and bad solutions than it would with a weaker generator.

**ORM overoptimisation is real and grows with n.** At n = 32 the ORM loses 1.50 pp from
its own peak. This is a well-known failure mode of outcome supervision when used for
reranking: the RM learns surface correlates of correct answers (e.g. crisp final
statements, appropriate length), not the actual reasoning quality, and solutions can
exploit these correlates at scale.

**PRM is more robust because every step is a bottleneck.** The min-step aggregation
prevents any single high-scoring step from masking a bad one. Even though outcome
propagation mislabels steps before an error as negative (§5 of the implementation page),
the PRM still learns a smoother ranking function that degrades gracefully at large n.

**Majority voting dominates both RMs.** Self-consistency at k = 32 (93.3% from E2.2)
outperforms PRM@32 (90.98%) by 2.3 pp without any RM training. For this model and task,
the agreement signal across independent samples is more informative than a 1.5B
classifier. This suggests the main bottleneck is RM quality (5,000 outcome-propagation
examples from a much stronger generator), not the reranking principle itself.

**The gap between outcome propagation and human labels drives the oracle gap.** The
correct answer is present in 98.12% of question pools, but neither RM closes more than
2.26 pp of the 9.40 pp headroom above k=1. Per-step human annotations (as in PRM800K)
would likely improve PRM substantially; outcome propagation labels nearly 100% of the
steps in almost-correct solutions incorrectly (see [implementation §5](E2.4_verifier_study.md#5-why-outcome-propagation-under-labels-the-prm)).

## 5. Comparison with E2.2

| | E2.2 | E2.4 |
|---|---:|---:|
| k=1 / single sample accuracy | 89.1% | 88.72% |
| Best reranking method (k=32) | SC@32: 93.3% | PRM@32: 90.98% |
| Oracle (any correct at k=32) | 98.0% | 98.12% |

The k=1 and oracle numbers are consistent across experiments (same model, same 300
questions set, slight variation from sampling). Self-consistency beats both RMs by 2–3 pp,
confirming that for Qwen2.5-7B on GSM8K the variance across independent samples is a
stronger reranking signal than a small-scale outcome-propagation RM.

## 6. Limitations

1. **266 of 300 eval questions** — 34 questions lost to OOM during the initial generation
   phase. Results are not directly paired with E2.1/E2.2 at the question level.
2. **Outcome propagation mislabels PRM** — steps in almost-correct solutions are labelled 0,
   teaching the PRM to distrust valid reasoning. Human step labels (PRM800K style) are needed
   for a fair comparison with Lightman et al.
3. **Weak RM backbone** (1.5B) relative to generator (7B) — the RM is evaluating reasoning
   produced by a model it cannot fully understand.
4. **Small training set** — 5,000 ORM and ~18,000 PRM examples; Lightman et al. used 800K
   human-labelled steps.
5. **Non-significant p-value** — the effect is real in direction but underpowered. Any
   conclusion about PRM > ORM quantitatively would require more eval questions or a
   weaker generator (lower baseline, more headroom).

## 7. Connection to multimodal reasoning

The core difficulty exposed here — obtaining per-step labels — becomes an order of magnitude
harder for images:

- **Text:** automatic outcome verification (is the numeric answer correct?) is straightforward
  and scales to millions of examples. Per-step verification is expensive but possible via
  human annotation or formal solvers.
- **Images:** there is no equivalent to `extract_pred` / `is_correct` for a diagram-understanding
  task. The "step" in a visual reasoning chain (what region to attend to, what property to
  extract) has no canonical textual form. Outcome propagation is essentially the only scalable
  labelling strategy, and the present experiment demonstrates how much accuracy it costs.

This motivates Stage 3's exploration of intermediate verification proxies (executable geometry
programs, region-grounded CoT) that can generate per-step supervision signals without human
annotation.
